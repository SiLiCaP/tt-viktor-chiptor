The demo on the table. The PC plays the RP2350: it runs ML-KEM and packs every coefficient into a pin word. The Tang Nano 20K FPGA runs the chip's hardened gate netlist, cycle for cycle. Every chip call goes over USB as three commands: LOAD the pin words into the FPGA's stimulus RAM, RUN them through the chip one clock each, then READ_CAP the output pins it captured on every clock. Click an action and watch the bytes move, in slow motion.

Why a key exchange takes seconds here: every pin word crosses USB twice at 1 Mbaud (2 bytes in, 3 bytes back), and the chip finishes each batch in a fraction of a millisecond. On the Tiny Tapeout board the RP2350 drives the pins directly with PIO and DMA at the chip's clock, so the same exchange is predicted at about 9 ms (tab 7).

A post-quantum coprocessor on a Tiny Tapeout tile. The RP2350 (here: the PC) runs ML-KEM; our chip does its polynomial arithmetic: forward NTT, inverse NTT, basemul and scale, modulo 3329. The strip above shows the chip's real pins, live: coefficients going in, result bytes coming out at a fixed delay.

Layer 1 of the NTT carries the secret polynomial in the clear. With masking, the RP2350 splits it into two random shares, so the pins only ever carry noise.

Tampering

How the finished product would work on the Tiny Tapeout demo board. The RP2350 runs ML-KEM and keeps every key and polynomial in its SRAM; one PIO state machine clocks our chip in lockstep while DMA streams the words. The timeline is one ML-KEM-512 key exchange, predicted from the chip's exact clock counts (50 MHz) and cited RP2350 cycle counts, played in slow motion.
